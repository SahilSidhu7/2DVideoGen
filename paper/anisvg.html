Vector animation · small models · consumer hardware

AniSVG: a token budget for generated animation

Text-to-video is priced in pixels. We re-price it in vector deltas — about six tokens per moving shape per frame — and train language models to write animation on a single 8 GB laptop GPU.

Sahil Sidhu · independent Working paper · rev. 24 August 2026
Hardware for every measurement: RTX 4060 Laptop (8 GB), 15.3 GB RAM, NVMe, Windows 11
Keywords vector animation · structured generation · constrained decoding · parameter-efficient fine-tuning · consumer-GPU training
Abstract

A language model cannot generate video frame-by-frame in pixels: the token cost is prohibitive. We measured a raster-tracing pipeline on real cartoon footage at roughly 60,000 tokens per second of video, which forecloses the approach for any model that fits on consumer hardware. We instead introduce AniSVG, a text representation in which a clip declares a cast of shapes once and each frame carries only integer delta operations. Measured with the Qwen3 tokenizer, AniSVG costs ~6 tokens per moving shape per frame — a median of 71 tokens per frame against ~4,000 for traced SVG, a 19× reduction — which brings a whole clip inside a small model's context.

We build a 91,387-clip corpus by converting Lottie animations, and fine-tune Qwen3-8B-Base with LoRA on one 8 GB GPU. Three findings generalise beyond this task. First, on a large-vocabulary model the loss, not the weights, bounds training: fp32 logits over a 151,936 vocabulary cost ~1.05 GB per 1,850-token sample, and chunking the cross-entropy cut peak VRAM 4.92 → 3.16 GB at identical loss. Second, layer streaming from NVMe is nearly free when the run is compute-bound — 315 vs 328 tok/s against an in-RAM store. Third, grammar-constrained decoding is not optional for structured output, and character-level masking is insufficient: valid-generation rate moved from 0% to 94% only once the mask operated on whole tokens.

Our central negative result separates what scale fixes from what it does not. Training a 1.7B model on 7× the animation signal of an 8B run lowered loss (0.435 → 0.271) and raised valid output to 100%, and it made colour and object count match the caption — but left geometry essentially unchanged: no generated clip depicts a recognisable object. The palette line and operation structure are short and repetitive and are learned; the vertex sequences are long runs of exact integers and are not. We attribute the gap to coordinate regression through next-token prediction rather than to model or corpus size, and argue for a hybrid in which the language model supplies motion and pose while a diffusion model supplies appearance, conditioned on guides that are exact rather than estimated.

Contributions

  3. The first published measurement of Soup's disk-tier layer streaming, previously documented as unmeasured: ~4% slower than an in-RAM store (§5).

01The problem is the token budget

Generative video is dominated by diffusion over pixels. That is not available to a small autoregressive model on a laptop, so the question becomes: is there a textual representation of moving pictures cheap enough for one to write?

We answered this empirically before designing anything. Public-domain cartoons were vectorised frame-by-frame with vtracer, then encoded as compactly as we could manage, and measured on 144 real traced frames:

The delta encoding is the informative row. It bought almost nothing because independently traced frames have no path correspondence — shape 4 in one frame is unrelated to shape 4 in the next, so there is nothing to subtract. Two further attempts failed for reasons worth recording: separating a static background from moving foreground scored 0.49× (worse than not doing it), because these cartoons pan almost constantly and ragged mask boundaries add path complexity; and the painted, textured backgrounds of the source material consumed roughly 80% of all traced points.

Negative result, recorded deliberately

Raster tracing was pursued to a measured dead end, not abandoned on intuition. We publish the numbers because the approach is the obvious first idea, and knowing its cost saves the next person the same weeks.

That measurement sets the target. A 0.6–8B model can realistically emit on the order of a few thousand coherent tokens, so a two- to four-second clip allows roughly 40–80 tokens per frame. That is not a painted scene. It is a small cast of shapes that move — which is what animation, as opposed to video, actually is.

02Related work

Vector generation. DeepSVG [1] models SVG hierarchically and showed latent interpolation between icons; OmniSVG [2] scales text-to-SVG to two million assets with a vision-language backbone, including anime characters. Both generate static graphics. Our concern is motion, and the distinction matters: a static generator can be excellent and still contribute nothing to animation, because it never represents time.

Vector animation data. Lottie/Bodymovin [3] is the dominant interchange format for shape animation on the web, and LottieGPT [4] released 660k captioned Lottie animations, which we convert. Lottie is a renderer format rather than a generation target: it is verbose, deeply nested, and its keyframe interpolation must be evaluated rather than read. AniSVG is the flattened, sampled view of the same information.

Training under memory limits. LoRA [5] and QLoRA [6] make a large base trainable on one GPU by freezing it; Soup's layer streaming [7] goes further and keeps the frozen base out of VRAM entirely. All three bound the weights. Cut Cross-Entropy [8] targets the term that actually dominates at large vocabulary — the logits — which is the same observation we arrive at independently in §5, and which we implement directly because the published integration no longer imports.

Conditioned image synthesis. ControlNet [9] conditions diffusion on pose or edge maps, which are normally estimated per frame by a detector. §7 argues that a vector clip supplies the same signals exactly and coherently, removing the estimator that is a known source of flicker.

03The AniSVG representation

AniSVG states a clip as a cast plus a sequence of edits. Shapes are declared once with their geometry; every subsequent frame lists only what changed. All values are integers, and a shape not mentioned in a frame holds its pose.

Motion is four numbers, not a vertex list

A shape usually moves far more than it changes. Each frame fits a similarity transform (translate, rotate, uniform scale) between the cast pose and the frame pose in closed form, so those four numbers absorb the bulk of the motion; only the residual becomes a per-vertex morph. This split is what makes the format cheap: a walking figure costs a few transform operations per frame instead of a full coordinate dump.

Quantisation-aware deltas

Every operation is rounded to an integer, so the encoder diffs against the pose the decoder will hold after replaying the rounded stream — not against the true pose. Without this, sub-pixel jitter emits a stream of no-op t id 0 0 operations and rounding error accumulates across the clip. This is a small detail that determines whether the format is actually compact in practice.

04Corpus: why Lottie

Tracing fails because it destroys shape correspondence. Lottie (Bodymovin) restores it for free: sampling one shape tree at successive times means shape k at frame t is shape k at frame t+1. That single property is why the conversion work is worth doing.

We convert LottieGPT/LottieAnimation-660K (CC-BY-NC-SA-4.0). The repository is 132 GB, but 129 GB of that is rendered MP4 previews that a vector pipeline never reads; only the 2.96 GB of metadata shards carry the Lottie JSON and captions. Shards are streamed over HTTP, decompressed and converted in flight, and discarded — peak disk cost is the gzipped output alone, ~0.8 KB per clip.

Two corrections that changed the corpus

Null layers (ty:3) were initially counted as unsupported, flagging two thirds of the corpus as lossy. Nulls hold no art at all — they exist to be parented to, and their transform was already being applied through the parent chain. Excluding them lifted the clean share from 38% to 62% of convertible clips.

Conversion, not download, is the bottleneck: 0.27 s/record single-threaded. Moving it to a process pool gave 5.2×, and rejecting on a single sampled frame before paying for a full conversion gave a further 13× — because cast size and unsupported features are both decidable from one frame, while conversion samples every frame.

Trim, do not drop

AniSVG frames are individually addressable lines and the header's frame count is derived from the frame list, so an over-long clip can be re-emitted with fewer frames. At a 4,096-token cap this moved clip loss from 37% to 11%: a six-second animation truncated to three is a far better trade than a discarded one. The packed corpus is 78,971 clips and 198.3M tokens, median 2,531 tokens per clip.

A second corpus, generated rather than collected

The converted corpus contains no characters, so no model trained on it can produce one. Rather than search for character animation that does not exist in vector form at scale, we generate it: an articulated rig maps an action and a phase to joint positions, and sampling it over time yields exactly the shape correspondence AniSVG requires — limb k is limb k at every frame — so procedural clips encode at the same cost as converted ones and can be mixed with them.

Two variants were produced, 20,000 clips each, balanced across six actions (walk, run, jump, kick, wave, dance) with zero failures: a line-art stick figure, and a cel-style figure with an oversized head, hair silhouette, large eyes, tapered limbs and clothing. The cel figure uses a fixed sixteen-part schema in fixed paint order, so shape index k is the same body part in every clip — including in generated output, which is what allows a pose skeleton to be recovered from what a model writes rather than only from what the generator produced (§7).

Captions are emitted from the parameters that produced the clip, so they are true by construction rather than inferred, a property no scraped corpus has. The cost is density: every limb moves every frame, giving a median of 3,110 tokens per clip against 2,531 for converted animations.

05What actually bounds an 8 GB GPU

The expectation going in was that model size would decide what we could train. It did not.

Every large configuration failed on the same 1.05 GiB allocation regardless of model size or context length — the signature of a term that depends on neither. Qwen3ForCausalLM upcasts logits to fp32 for the loss, so a single 1,850-token sample against a 151,936-token vocabulary materialises ~1.05 GB, and its gradient the same again. On a small model with a large vocabulary, the loss is the largest tensor in the step.

Scoring the sequence in slices, so the full logit tensor never exists, removes it. The objective is unchanged — built-in, manual and chunked cross-entropy agree to four decimals (0.8340) on a fixed batch:

Implementation trap

Transformers 4.51+ passes num_items_in_batch into compute_loss and then does not divide by the accumulation steps itself. A custom loss returning a plain mean inflates every gradient by exactly the accumulation factor. It presented as train loss 2.678 against 0.675 at accumulation 4 — and the ratio is the tell.

Streaming from disk is nearly free

Our host had insufficient free RAM to pin the 3.58 GB NF4 store, so the 8B run fell back to streaming decoder layers from NVMe — each read twice per step, once in the forward pass and once when the backward pass recomputes it. The published benchmark for this tier notes its slowdown was unmeasured. We measured it: 315 tok/s from disk against 328 tok/s from RAM, a ~4% penalty, because the run is compute-bound rather than I/O-bound (GPU utilisation held at 92–98%).

Measurement caution

Our first estimate of this penalty was 4.5×, computed by dividing total steps by elapsed-since-process-start. Roughly an hour of that window was one-time tokenisation and shard preparation. Rates for long runs must be measured across step boundaries in steady state, never from process start.

06Decoding is where structured generation is won

After a full epoch (28,034 clips, 1,024 context, 29.8 h, loss 0.86 → 0.40) the model wrote well-formed AniSVG — correct headers, palettes, cast declarations with plausible vertex deltas. Its valid-generation rate was 0%.

Every point of that gap was in decoding and evaluation, not in the weights. The sequence of causes is the useful part of this section:

The prompt prefix must match training exactly

Qwen3's chat template emits an empty reasoning block before the answer, so every training example ran …assistant\n<think>\n\n</think>\n\nH 256 …. The conventional add_generation_prompt=True stops at the assistant marker, leaving the model to invent that block. Sampling sometimes stumbled through it; greedy never did. Rather than hardcode the block, render a sentinel assistant message and cut the string at it — this recovers the exact prefix whatever the template does.

Character-level masking is insufficient

The two failure modes are complementary: less randomness removes corruption and variety; more randomness restores variety and corruption. The resolution is to make invalid tokens unreachable rather than unlikely, so sampling can supply variety without being able to break syntax.

A naive character filter is not enough. AniSVG's hex digits (a–f) and operation letters (t r s o v h w) share an alphabet that spells real words, so tokens such as edores, errar and .Dao pass a per-character test and land inside a number. The mask must accept or reject whole tokens: a run of digits, one structural character, or a short hex group. That change alone moved 75% → 94%, retaining 7,970 of 151,669 tokens.

Evaluation must mean renderable

Loss is a poor guide for a structured format. Our scorer initially called a clip valid if it parsed — including clips that crashed on replay. Validity must be established by replaying every operation against the cast and rasterising. A clip can also be structurally perfect and draw nothing: generated shapes under greedy decoding had a median extent of 0×0 against 15–172 for ground truth. Metrics for generated structure should measure the rendered result, not the emitted symbols.

07Where a vector-only approach stops

The representation has a ceiling, and it is worth stating precisely because it is easy to misattribute. The limit is not SVG: SVG supports gradients, masks, filters and blur. AniSVG excludes them, and it excludes them for one reason - token cost. The real constraint is that a small autoregressive model can afford roughly 40-80 tokens per frame, and visual style is expensive in tokens while motion is cheap.

That suggests splitting the problem along its own grain rather than extending the format: let the language model write motion, which is cheap and which it can be made to write correctly, and let a diffusion model paint style, which is expensive in tokens but free in pixels.

The conditioning signal is exact, not estimated

ControlNet conditioning is normally recovered from a rendered image by a preprocessor - a detector guessing joint positions, an edge filter guessing lines. That estimate is a source of error, and because it is recomputed independently per frame it is a source of flicker, which is the dominant failure mode in diffusion-based animation.

A vector clip removes the estimator entirely. The cast is declared once and the frames are deltas on it, so limb k is limb k at every timestep by construction. Fixing the part schema - the same sixteen parts, in the same order, in every clip - means a skeleton can be recovered from generated output as well as from the generator, by index rather than by inference. Nothing is detected, so nothing can be detected differently from one frame to the next.

Open question, reported before the outcome is known

Whether per-frame diffusion conditioned on exact vector guides yields temporally stable anime is not yet established here. Exact conditioning removes preprocessor flicker, but it does not by itself constrain texture, shading or hair between frames, and those are free to vary under the sampler. This section states the design and its rationale; the measurement follows, and will be reported whether or not it works.

08Results: what scale fixed, and what it did not

Two models were trained on the same converted corpus, differing in size and in how much of each clip they saw. The 8B ran under layer streaming at a 1,024-token context, admitting 29k of 79k clips at ~10 frames each; the 1.7B ran resident at 4,096 tokens, taking all 77,397 clips at ~25 frames each — roughly 7× the animation signal per epoch.

The qualitative difference is specific and repeatable. Given "two four-pointed star-shaped figures, a larger purple one and a smaller pink one", v2 emits a large pink form and a small purple one: the palette is right, the count is right, the relative sizes are right, and neither form is a star. Given "a blue and a red speech bubble" it emits exactly two shapes, one blue and one red. v1, by contrast, produced near-monochrome scribbles.

The finding

The two halves of an AniSVG clip are learned at very different rates. The palette line and the operation structure are short, low-entropy and highly repetitive, and they are learned well. The vertex sequences are long runs of precise integers in which a single wrong digit deforms the shape, and they are barely learned at all. Multiplying the training signal by seven moved colour fidelity substantially and geometry almost not at all.

We therefore attribute the depiction gap to coordinate regression through next-token prediction rather than to model size or corpus size. That is a property of the interface between the task and the architecture, not a shortage of compute — and it is the result that motivates §7.

Limitations

No generated clip depicts a recognisable object. The corpus is motion-graphics iconography, so the learned motion vocabulary is rotation and translation rather than articulated movement. The source licence is non-commercial. The representation cannot express gradients, image layers or trim-path reveals, which is why 17% of source animations are rejected rather than approximated. Every number here comes from a single machine and a single seed.

09Negative results, in full

Each of these cost real time, and each is reported because the approach is plausible enough that someone else will try it.

Raster tracing of real footage (§1)

~57,000-60,000 tokens per second of video after every compaction we could devise. Inter-frame delta encoding bought ~2% because independently traced frames share no path identity. Separating static background from moving foreground scored 0.49× — worse than not doing it — because the source cartoons pan almost constantly and mask boundaries add path complexity.

Greedy decoding collapses this format

Greedy produced 13 of 15 shapes identical at zero extent — clips that parse, replay and render as a blank canvas. Sampling produced good geometry and corrupted numbers. The two decoders fail in opposite directions, so no temperature setting fixes both; only constraining the vocabulary does.

Character-level vocabulary masking is insufficient

AniSVG's hex digits (a-f) and operation letters (t r s o v h w) share an alphabet that spells words, so edores, errar and .Dao pass a per-character filter and land inside numbers. 75% valid; token-level masking reached 94%.

Cut Cross-Entropy cannot be switched on

The intended fix for the logits bound is unusable on any current stack: its transformers integration imports private symbols (_CONFIG_FOR_DOC) that modern versions no longer export, and it eagerly imports a gemma2 patcher, so a non-gemma model trips a gemma2 incompatibility [8]. We reimplemented the idea instead (§5).

The hybrid, first measurement: style yes, control no, stability no

We rendered a procedural clip's exact pose guides through SD1.5 with ControlNet-OpenPose, one fixed seed and identical settings on every frame, so that the only thing varying between frames was the conditioning image. Measured as mean absolute inter-frame change:

One half of the hybrid worked immediately: the output is genuine anime - a cel-style character with large eyes, hair and clothing, at a quality the token budget could never buy. The other half failed twice, and the two failures have different causes.

The pose was not followed. The guides show an arm raised in a wave; the rendered characters stand with hands on hips. Our skeleton is OpenPose-like - limb axes recovered from the part schema and drawn in the OpenPose palette - but it is not the COCO-18 keypoint topology the ControlNet was trained on: it has no explicit neck, nose, eye or ear keypoints, and its joints are limb endpoints rather than named body points. The conditioning was therefore weak enough to be largely ignored. This is an implementation defect on our side, not a limit of the method, and it is fixable precisely because the rig gives exact joints.

Identity was not preserved. Every frame is a different character - different hair length, different face, and a background that reorganises completely. This is the expected behaviour of per-frame diffusion with no temporal mechanism, and a fixed seed does not address it: the seed fixes the starting noise, not the trajectory the sampler takes once the conditioning changes. Exact conditioning removes preprocessor flicker, which was the claim in §7, but preprocessor flicker was evidently not the dominant term.

Correction to §7

§7 argued that exact conditioning would buy temporal stability because it removes the per-frame estimator. That argument was stated before measurement and is wrong as stated. Removing estimator flicker is necessary but nowhere near sufficient; the sampler's own freedom dominates by more than an order of magnitude. Any route to stable output needs an explicit temporal mechanism - a motion module, latent chaining between frames, or cross-frame attention - not merely a better guide.

Two of our own measurement errors

We first computed the disk-tier penalty as 4.5× by dividing total steps by elapsed-since-process-start; roughly an hour of that window was one-time tokenisation and shard preparation. Measured across step boundaries in steady state it is ~4%. Separately, a throughput estimate taken from a probe on 2048-token data under-predicted a 4096-token run by 1.9×. Both errors share a cause: extrapolating from a window that is not the run.

The corpus does not contain what we wanted

LottieAnimation-660K is motion-graphics iconography. A model trained on it produces animated icons however well it is trained — no amount of scale yields characters, because characters are absent from the data. We report this because "train longer" is the default response to weak output, and here it would have been wrong.

10Reproducibility

Every number in this paper was measured on one machine: an RTX 4060 Laptop (8 GB), 15.3 GB host RAM, NVMe, Windows 11, with PyTorch 2.13+cu126 and Transformers 4.51/4.57. Windows figures are systematically pessimistic against Linux, and expandable_segments — which was nonetheless necessary to reach 4,096 context at 0.6B — is silently ignored on some Windows configurations.

11What transfers

Three results are independent of animation and apply to anyone training a modern large-vocabulary model on a small GPU, or generating any strict format:

  1. Profile the loss, not just the weights. With a 151,936-token vocabulary, fp32 logits and their gradient can exceed everything else in the step. If an out-of-memory error recurs at the same size regardless of model or sequence length, the cause depends on neither.
  2. Verify the prompt prefix against real training text. Chat templates insert content — an empty reasoning block, in our case — that add_generation_prompt does not reproduce. Greedy decoding is unforgiving of the mismatch, and it presents as a model failure.
  3. Constrain decoding at the token level, and score on the artefact. For strict grammars, masking makes malformed output impossible rather than improbable — but only if the mask reasons about whole tokens. And a structured-output metric should render the result: parsing proves nothing about whether anything was drawn.
