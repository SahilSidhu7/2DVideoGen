We report twenty-three logged attempts to build a small, locally trainable 2D animation system on one 8GB consumer laptop GPU, with an evaluation harness built to judge results honestly. The first half pursues styled character animation via Stable Diffusion 1.5 + ControlNet + AnimateDiff and closes that route by measurement: even after correcting a topology bug, isolating a deeper proportion mismatch, and adding the first mechanism to reduce flicker at all, every configuration remains a hard failure, and the fix that helps flicker costs pose obedience. The second half changes direction: a CPU-only symbolic scene-script compositor produces the project's first multi-character animation and clears the evaluation gates by a wide margin. A 60M-parameter model then learns to write these scripts from natural language, training in minutes on CPU, but shows a large compositional generalization gap on cast size, traced by a controlled probe to a spurious correlation in the project's own training data; deconfounding cuts it substantially. The third produces the headline finding: "poorly staged" scenes attributed to the model are mostly a decoding artefact. Sampling instead of beam search recovers most staging quality on identical weights, and an ablation shows a spacing pass would otherwise have been mistaken for the fix - good engineering, bad science claim. Style, motion, identity and pose trade against each other rather than compounding, and an apparent model defect can itself be a decoding artefact, each caught by a control that could have shown the opposite. We answer the project's own indictment - "the models never beat the script" - directly: they still do not on richness, but a model-written scene now stages better than the weaker of two hand-written reference clips, while still failing to obey stated spatial instructions above chance. The model composes; it does not yet take direction.
